Wrong options are engineered to tempt

The strongest candidates learn to resist the trap.

In Safety items, the difference is often not knowing the right answer but resisting the wrong one. The tempting option is built to feel reasonable.

When you can name why it tempts, it loses much of its pull. This resource helps you identify the trap behind an explanation and collect the most common Safety and Infection Control traps to keep beside you while you practice.

The naming move

Name the trap in three steps

1
Find the distractor
Read the explanation and identify the wrong option most candidates choose, not only the correct answer.
2
Ask why it tempts
Does it look more active, feel safer, follow an old habit, or skip a required step?
3
Name the trap
Create a short phrase you will recognize next time, such as restraint reflex or droplet-for-airborne.
Modeled naming 1

Transmission precautions

Sample item: A client is admitted with suspected pulmonary tuberculosis. Which precaution should the nurse set up first?
A. Place the client in a private room and use standard precautions
B. Place the client in a negative-pressure room and wear an N95 respirator
C. Place the client on contact precautions and wear a gown and gloves
D. Place the client on droplet precautions and wear a surgical mask
B is correct because tuberculosis is airborne and requires a negative-pressure room and an N95 respirator. Standard precautions alone are insufficient, contact precautions address spread by touch, and droplet precautions with a surgical mask do not stop airborne particles.
The option that pulls most candidates is D. A respiratory illness feels like droplet, and a surgical mask feels like enough. That feeling is the trap.
Named trap: droplet-for-airborne. Reaching for droplet precautions when the organism is airborne. The expected U.S. standard uses airborne precautions, a negative-pressure room, and an N95 respirator for tuberculosis.
Modeled naming 2

Fall prevention

Sample item: An older adult client at risk for falls becomes restless during the night. What should the nurse do first?
A. Apply a vest restraint to keep the client in bed
B. Raise all four side rails
C. Lower the bed, place the call light within reach, and increase rounding
D. Ask a family member to stay overnight
C is correct because least restrictive measures come first. A restraint is not a first-line fall intervention, four raised side rails function as a restraint, and relying on a family member is not a dependable nursing intervention.
The options that tempt are A and B. A restraint feels protective and decisive, which is exactly why candidates reach for it.
Named trap: the restraint reflex. Choosing a restraint, including four raised side rails, when a less restrictive intervention is correct.

Read defensively
Ask what the tempting option is quietly inviting you to do: overreact, skip a step, trust an unsafe assumption, or use a stronger intervention than necessary.
Protect without adding risk
Match precautions to transmission route, use least restrictive measures, verify anything that seems unsafe, and stay with the client in an emergency.
